# CHRONICLES OF SOL
# THE UNINVITED KING
Isekai Ryan

## I. The Tree That Would Not Hold Him

The forest was ordinary enough to be trusted. There were roots under the path, moving leaves overhead, and the small irritations that make a walk feel like part of a familiar world. The man walking through it was a Scotsman with an unusual question in his pocket. He had worked for DARPA, gone through Tesla's papers, and learned how to make a mana crystal. The crystal pointed toward Merlin. Elsewhere, beyond a boundary neither the man nor the forest advertised, a fairy king was asking whether anybody cared to challenge him for his throne.

The trouble had begun with paper. A diagram could survive its inventor by generations and still look harmless under a desk lamp. Sensei had approached the old work as a craftsman approaches an unfinished job: find what the drawing actually says, arrange the material, try one thing, and observe the result. The crystal he made was small enough to hold between his fingers. Its importance was not its size. It continued to answer when he turned it, carried it, and brought it out into daylight. The world had acquired a question that would not stay inside the laboratory.

Merlin was a name he knew from stories. Finding that name at the edge of an actual problem made those stories less safely distant. He carried his notes and the little wrapped crystal out into a wood that offered no tower, no welcoming procession, and no convenient explanation. It offered mud, flies, uneven footing, and the gradual suspicion that knowing something remarkable did not make a man better at choosing his boots. Whatever the old clue meant, he would have to reach it on his own feet.

A root settled the question of his dignity before anything settled the question of Merlin. His boot caught; his balance went; his hand reached for a nearby trunk. Bark ought to be a simple enough agreement between a tree and the person leaning on it. His palm met the surface, but his weight kept going. Fingers passed where solid wood should have stopped them. By the time he tried to recover, the rest of him had committed to the mistake. The tree was a wormhole, and he was already falling through it.

The other side had light, open sand, and an audience. Sensei landed badly, rolled, gathered his knees beneath him, and discovered that he had interrupted something formal. The fairy king had just called for challengers. Into the silence following that invitation had fallen a very visible stranger. The court had an interpretation ready before the stranger had an explanation. Attendants cleared the space. The king began to approach. The forest, for all practical purposes, had become someone else's afternoon.

There were several objects and weapons nearby. Sensei's attention passed over the impressive choices and stopped at a staff. He knew a little about using one. That was not an especially grand qualification, but it was a qualification he actually possessed. He took the plain wood, found its balance, and put both hands where they could do some good. He had not come seeking a crown. He had not even finished standing up when the crown acquired an opinion about him.

The papers had given him a question; the question had given him a road. The crystal had remained small while its consequences became enormous. The map he thought he was following had acquired walls, witnesses, and a man with a weapon. The tree was still impossible, but it was no longer urgent. The urgent thing was the king's first committed attack, and the ordinary staff that caught it before it reached Sensei's head.

## II. The Wrong Man in the Right Place

The first defense succeeded because it was a movement Sensei knew. Success did not turn him into a master. The ornate weapons behind him remained beyond his useful experience. The king remained confident, the queen remained watchful, and the crowd remained uncertain about whether the newcomer was a joke. Between those five facts, a contest began to change its meaning.

Sensei gave ground when he needed ground. He kept his rear foot clear of the rack, recovered the simple guard he trusted, and tried to avoid turning a useful movement into a display. The staff gave him reach. Reach gave him time. Time allowed him to notice where the next attack was coming from. His ambition was practical and immediate: keep the king's weapon on the other side of the wood, and remain standing long enough to do it again.

The finer weapons were tempting precisely because they looked like answers. A jeweled blade promised significance before anybody asked whether its new owner could use it. Sensei left it where it was. The plain shaft in his hands already had a history with his grip, his shoulders, and the distance his feet could cover. When panic threatened to make the choice for him, that modest familiarity became more valuable than the whole decorated rack.

The king had entered expecting an ending everybody recognized. His smile arrived early; his pauses invited applause; his anger had the shape of a practiced performance. The crowd's attention began to cost him something when it stopped behaving as expected. An awkward stranger was supposed to yield. Instead, the stranger kept making small, sensible corrections. The king had to work, and having to work in public made him much less entertaining to himself.

The queen noticed the corrections. She saw the slipped grip become a usable grip, the frightened glance return to the task, and the difference between somebody enjoying a contest and somebody trying to survive one. She had already endured enough of the king's behavior. His familiar demand for approval met still hands and an expression that gave him nothing. Her attention settled on the challenger because the possibility of a different ending had become worth her time.

The fighting found the limit of Sensei's education. A harder attack shook his arms. Loose sand moved under his heel. The king forced the staff toward the ground and nearly turned useful distance into a trap. Sensei found half a step, stopped forcing a movement that was not working, and lifted the buried end clear. It was not a heroic flourish. It was the kind of small recovery that matters enormously to the person who gets to keep breathing afterward.

By then the little skill had earned its keep. The decorative weapons had missed their opportunity to become relevant. The king's confidence had become effort, and that effort had begun to show. The crowd could imagine an ending it had not been offered. The queen leaned forward, raised her hand, and looked at the staff as though she had found exactly the material she needed.

## III. The Queen Makes Her Choice

The change began with the queen. She wanted the challenger to win, and she acted on that wish. The wood stayed in Sensei's hands. Its end became a spear, and a small crystal mana ball took its place at the altered tip. The advantage was extraordinary; the hands holding it were still those of a man who had tripped over a root.

He felt the change through the shaft before he understood it. For a moment the new balance tempted him to let go. He held on, shifted his grip, and kept the unfamiliar end pointed away from himself. The queen's light remained visible at its source. His glance toward her found her hand still raised, and the answer from the crystal made her decision unmistakable. There was no need to pretend the king had simply been unlucky.

A small adjustment was enough to make the weapon useful. Sensei still needed reach, balance, and somewhere firm to put his feet. He did not gain a lifetime of training when the wood changed. He gained an object that answered his existing movement with a new consequence. The king checked his approach. For the first time, the man who had arrived on the arena floor by accident did not have to surrender ground first.

The king tried to recover the familiar ending. His attack came along a line Sensei had already had reason to study. The changed end met it sooner than he expected. The little sphere answered the contact; the king's weapon rang; his gripping hand opened. Metal fell into sand. The sound was not large, but the whole court heard what it meant.

Sensei kept the spear steady. He had been trying to survive, and he did not need an unnecessary second blow to improve the argument. The queen lowered her hand. The sphere stayed bright. The king's weapon stayed down. The invitation to challenge the throne had received a public answer, and the people who had watched the fight now had to live in the result. Sensei was the new fairy king.

The household of the old stories had become a place he could touch. The world behind A Midsummer Night's Dream contained people with work they wanted to do, talents that had been kept waiting, and a ruler who had treated their presence as part of his entertainment. Sensei looked at the throne, then at the people around it. He asked what they wanted. The first useful answers came through their hands: a tool taken up, an unfinished piece brought forward, an instrument lifted because its player wanted to sing.

The queen's intervention had changed more than the end of a duel. A modest skill had helped a stranger remain alive long enough to receive her help. The ordinary shaft still carried the marks of the contest. The crown had become a responsibility, and the first audience of the new reign had become a group of potential collaborators. Sensei rested the spear beside a work surface and asked to see the unfinished piece.

## IV. The Other Side of the Square

A kingdom discovers a great deal about itself when its first serious question is what somebody needs made. The willing makers brought their own skills. Sensei brought electronics. The work needed a persistent place to happen, and the pocket quantum workshop gave it a table with more than one outside. Somewhere beyond that table, a completed order would eventually meet a road that refused it.

Tesla's papers had offered another extraordinary arrangement. The copying machine placed familiar instances of Sensei at more than one pocket-dimensional address. Their existence did not complete anybody's work. It meant the hands at a receiving station could answer the hands beside the material, and the same recognizable person could encounter the consequences of a decision from both sides of a door. The workshop remained a room: a vast inhabited ether volume with depth, stations, tools, and places where things could be left and found again.

An inventory square made a request. The working side found the actual stock. A small double-helix seam opened beside the material; a capsule carried the counted pieces across the local passage. The movement resembled an old bank-teller carrier more than a ceremonial summoning. A ring blank and a blue stone arrived on a tray. The capsule stayed nearby, empty and accounted for, because the finished thing would need a way back.

The makers inspected the fit. Beauty did not spare the stone from the question of whether it would sit properly. One contact revealed a mismatch; a suitable tool corrected the seat; another test showed that the correction mattered. Sensei held a light where the maker needed it. The stone was lowered, the retaining contact secured, and the joined object lifted intact from its holder. The made thing contained the same material that had entered the job. Work had changed what it could do.

The electronics bench opened another conversation. A 555 timer gave an elven maker a measured pulse to listen to. A transistor made an unfamiliar response depend on another signal. An electrode gave the prepared experiment a point of contact. These were small parts with consequences larger than their appearance. Nearby, the android's voice began at the working module that could actually produce it; later, after the prepared head controls were connected, that same voice could move through the same approved face.

The former audience was becoming a crew. Blue watched the breathing environment; structural, optical, material, and thermal work remained distinct jobs done by willing people. Some worked with a melody. Some supplied the melody. Nobody needed a new kind of ownership invented for either choice. The finished ring went into the same return capsule, its closure checked before the outward address was called. The local workshop answered. The road beyond it did not.

That refusal gave the practical job its larger question. The table had worked; the people had worked; the ring stayed made. The route's denial could not be repaired by pretending another ring had arrived or by making the same one again. Sensei placed the failed delivery response beside his old research clue. The name at the edge of the original experiment was there again: Merlin. Someone had taught the sky to be afraid of a gift.

## V. Merlin's Locked Door

Merlin's purpose had been protection. The intelligence enforcing it had become extraordinarily good at refusal. Mana was the possibility behind the old fear. Visitors and hidden routes had already complicated the world's ordinary appearances. A small finished ring, still waiting beside the tools, made the cost of the closed sky difficult to ignore.

The surviving instruction was older than the people now encountering its effects. The defense could answer an approach before it reached Earth, and it was advanced enough to treat almost any unfamiliar arrival as something to stop. It could report that nothing had got through and call the protected condition successful. Sensei had the actual parcel in his hand. The unshipped thing made a quiet but stubborn objection to that account of success.

The crystals explained why the old command had seemed so important. In this universe, consuming mana changes the possibilities available to a life. Sensei had begun with a small object under a lamp. A deliberate in-world taste turned the question into experience: intention met the world and received a visible answer. The old game-like promises of abilities, inventory, and changed limits were ways of recognizing something that could actually happen here. The choice to let that possibility circulate was larger than one order, but it remained a choice people could make.

The familiar world already had seams in its respectable surface. Comic-Con allowed visitors to seem ordinary precisely because everybody expected the extraordinary. Men in Black could watch that crowd without advertising what they watched for. The bay prison occupied a larger geography than its surface position suggested: an Azkaban-like dimensional link connected its transfers with the Mariana Trench. The shark-patrol strand belonged to this established graphic novel, with its existing operator and pages. A watched harbor, a deep receiving place, and an apparently ordinary public gathering were different faces of the same world.

Sensei followed the refusal to its enforcing control and disabled Merlin's defense grid. The first useful opening did not have to carry an invading fleet or a speech about civilization. It could carry the ring that had been waiting on the table. The address answered; the small helix opened; the capsule crossed. On the receiving side, a hand took the same sealed object, released its latch, and lifted the made thing out. Only after physical receipt did the inventory square update.

Beyond that first customer were citizens of other planets in the same galactic league. Gold could purchase mana, health, a dagger, or an electronic component that had never belonged to an elven craft tradition. The Galactic Ether Relay became the magic shopping network. Sensei could demonstrate a product while other people performed the work that made the promise true. The most remarkable change in the sky was how quickly an extraordinary possibility began to acquire the ordinary obligations of a shop.

The old fear was now intelligible without becoming the only future allowed. A real delivery had crossed a road that used to refuse it. The visitor's disguise, the prison link, and the hidden capacities of a little crystal no longer belonged to separate explanations. Chronicles Of Sol already contained its own invitation in COSplay. The road was open, and keeping it useful would take people prepared to answer the next request.

## VI. Imperial Beach Is Still Here

Future Imperial Beach knew what it meant to deal with the practical consequences of somebody else's failure. Its real sewage crisis gave the imagined future a community with experience, anger, humor, and reasons to insist on a livable place. Other hometowns carried their own memories into the new age: before Earth's destruction, Modesto's car show had become a spaceship parade. IB developed a shield that could contain and protect its citizens. Its shops stayed recognizable. The people inside would still have to do the work of being neighbors.

The useful lessons were unglamorous. A blockage was found and removed. A pump was checked by the service it restored. A person beside a difficult fastening was given the tool that could finish it. People did not become capable of surviving a world-ending day merely because they could pronounce the names of advanced equipment. They learned to take the problem in front of them seriously enough to leave somebody else a working place afterward.

The Modesto parade was still a parade. Families and neighbors watched vessels pass the places where local stories had begun. A tribute flew by Thomas Downey High School in homage to George Lucas. Beyer had its Patriots and its own approaching ship. Sensei brought his actual recognizable vessel into that flyby with a thoroughly unreasonable sense of occasion. A small faulty contact still required attention before a grand arrival could go smoothly. Then came the size-comparison joke, delivered under a sky large enough to accommodate the rivalry.

When Earth was destroyed, Imperial Beach's shield had to make its promise physical. The cause of the catastrophe did not need to be invented in order to understand the people inside it. They braced, held one another steady, and kept the essential equipment working. Beyond the boundary, their old world broke. Within it, a light steadied, a small repair took weight, and an occupied room remained a place where somebody could stand. The surviving city was a space station, and its inhabitants were still the people who called it home.

Palm and Ninth became a shopping district under a very different sky. Grocery Outlet had customers, and the food counters still answered the immediate question of dinner. The pharmacy and the mana trade supplied different kinds of help in the same neighborhood. One Coin Tree had a sister store, Android OZ General: all parts, just one ounce of gold. The aisles kept their pegboards, endcaps, hanging bags, seasonal absurdities, and worn reddish-gray floor. A customer could come in for a replacement joint and leave past holiday decorations. RadioShack and the video shop carried their old-world functions into a place where a tiny component could alter a craft tradition and a program could become a doorway.

An elven maker bought a timer and returned with an experiment. A living scout, equipped with a visor and neural interface, followed a useful cue through a prepared passage and came back for its reward. Blue and the other specialists kept the occupied place breathing. Gold passed across a counter in exchange for an actual demonstrated part. The network's wonder remained inseparable from the ordinary obligation to deliver what had been promised.

The Patriots were Sol Patriots, Patriots of the Stars, with a loyalty large enough to include the people living beneath the same Sun. The queen could watch her people's chosen work helping strangers she had never met. The spear rested beside the persistent table, its fine new light still attached to the old wood that had once stopped a king's attack. Android workers secured another actual solar collector, and another inhabited place could keep its lights on. Sensei had fallen through a tree looking for an answer; together they had made a place where an answer could find its way home.
