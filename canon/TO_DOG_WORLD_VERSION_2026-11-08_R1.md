### Submitted passage B — von Neumann history

Von Neumann probes are theoretical self-replicating spacecraft that use local materials to make copies of themselves and send those copies onward. The idea comes directly from John von Neumann’s work on self-reproducing machines in the late 1940s.
Origins (1940s–1960s)
In lectures at the University of Illinois in 1948–1949, von Neumann asked what minimum complexity a machine needs before it can replicate and then evolve the way living organisms do. He designed an abstract machine with three parts:
	•	A stored description (blueprint) of itself.
	•	A universal constructor that can read any description and build the machine it specifies.
	•	A copier that can duplicate the description.
Given its own description, the constructor builds a new copy and passes the description along. Stanislaw Ulam suggested modeling this in a cellular automaton instead of physical parts floating in a container; that became the better-known version. The full design was published after von Neumann’s death as Theory of Self-Reproducing Automata (1966), edited by Arthur W. Burks.
Around the same period, other people explored simpler physical versions. Lionel Penrose built mechanical models that could assemble copies of themselves. Edward Moore, Konrad Zuse, and later Freeman Dyson also wrote about self-replicating systems.
From theory to space probes
The jump to interstellar probes is straightforward: a single seed craft is sent to another star system. It finds asteroids, moons, or other raw material, builds more copies of itself, and launches those copies toward new stars. Because growth is exponential, one probe can, in principle, explore a galaxy on timescales of a few million years or less under optimistic assumptions (Frank Tipler and others popularized this argument in the 1970s–80s).
The same logic applies inside a single solar system. A seed factory placed on the Moon, in the asteroid belt, or in the Oort Cloud can grow by using local ice and rock. Once it has enough mass and energy, it can manufacture whatever infrastructure is needed—habitats, more probes, or, in the story you’re building, the first farming layer on a newly atmosphered Titan-class world.
Later refinements
	•	1980s–90s: K. Eric Drexler’s Engines of Creation brought the idea into nanotechnology (“clanking replicators”). Robert Freitas and Ralph Merkle produced the most thorough engineering survey of kinematic self-replicating machines.
	•	Practical limits: Full closure (making every component, including semiconductors and precision optics, from raw extraterrestrial material) is still hard. Near-term concepts usually aim for partial replication—70 % or more of the mass made on-site, with the hardest parts carried as “vitamins.”
	•	Doubling time: The speed of expansion is set by how fast a new copy can be built and launched. With good in-situ resource utilization, doubling times of months to a few years are plausible once the seed is established. That turns solar-system-scale growth into a matter of decades to centuries rather than millennia.
Relevance to the story you’re sketching
The Autophage / single-persistent-identity model you described is a direct descendant of von Neumann’s universal constructor. One pattern (the “guy”) arrives as a seed, prints successive bodies, and keeps rewriting the next page of the construction plan—exactly the way the constructor carries and copies its own description. Starting from the Oort Cloud or from a seed on Titan, the first robot dog or the first farmer instance is just the same pattern printed into a new body once the local resources and atmosphere stage allow it.
The history is therefore short and clear: von Neumann formalized the logic in the late 1940s, the space-probe application followed in the following decades, and the engineering problems that remain are mostly about closing the manufacturing loop with local materials. Everything else (galaxy-scale exploration, solar-system industrialization, or one persistent identity bootstrapping a Gaia layer on a manufactured world) is an application of that same exponential construction principle.

### Submitted passage C — power relay constellation

A solar-system power relay constellation in the next 10–100 years is not a Dyson sphere or interstellar probe. It is a practical network of solar collectors, power-beaming nodes, and relays that move energy where it is needed inside the solar system.
What is actually happening now and in the next decade
Space-based solar power (SBSP) and power beaming are moving from lab demos to small orbital systems.
	•	Caltech’s SSPD-1 (2023) already demonstrated space-to-space and space-to-ground power beaming elements.
	•	Multiple companies (Aetherflux, Overview Energy, Space Solar, StarCatcher, others) are planning constellation-style systems. Several aim for first meaningful beams in the late 2020s to early 2030s — kilowatts to megawatts to specific users (Antarctic bases, data centers, remote sites, or other satellites).
	•	China has published roadmaps targeting demonstration missions before 2030 and larger systems later. Japan and European efforts are on similar timelines.
	•	Laser and microwave beaming are both being tested. Lasers are better for focused, longer-range or satellite-to-satellite links; microwaves are better for larger ground receivers and higher power.
These early systems are mostly Earth-orbit focused, but the same technologies (lightweight solar arrays, efficient transmitters, beam pointing, and small satellite constellations) scale outward.
What a solar-system power relay network looks like by mid-century
Think of a sparse but interconnected grid of nodes rather than a continuous shell:
	1	Near-Earth and cislunar nodes (2030s)
GEO and medium-Earth-orbit collectors that beam to Earth or to other satellites. Lunar surface towers and rover-deployed nodes (concepts already exist for 2–20 kW modular stations with long-duration storage and local relays). These give continuous power to polar bases that otherwise spend half the time in darkness.
	2	Lagrange-point and heliocentric relays (2040s–2060s)
A small number of power and communications relays at Earth-Moon and Sun-Earth Lagrange points, plus a few in solar orbits between Earth and Mars. They do not generate most of the power; they receive a beam from a sunward collector and retransmit it farther out. This avoids the worst inverse-square losses and keeps line-of-sight when planets are on the far side of the Sun.
	3	Local generation + beamed top-up (Mars, asteroids, outer moons)
Each major site still has its own solar arrays, nuclear, or local ISRU power. The constellation supplies extra power during dust storms, long nights, or high-demand periods, and keeps the communication and navigation network alive. On Mars this is an extension of today’s relay satellites; on the Moon it is an extension of the modular solar towers already being designed.
Power levels stay modest by planetary standards — tens to hundreds of megawatts total across the network for a long time, not terawatts. The value is reliability and reach, not raw planetary energy.
What this actually enables in the next hundred years
	•	Continuous power and high-bandwidth links for lunar bases and early Mars surface operations without waiting for local nuclear plants.
	•	Power and data to asteroid-mining or processing craft that are too small to carry large power systems of their own.
	•	A backbone that lets a seed factory or remote scout on Titan (or elsewhere) stay in contact and receive occasional high-power top-ups instead of relying solely on RTGs or local solar.
	•	The same beam infrastructure that moves energy can also move data at higher rates than today’s Deep Space Network, because the relays are already pointed and powered.
It does not require self-replicating factories, kilometer-scale structures, or galactic expansion. It requires continued drops in launch cost, better lightweight solar and beaming hardware, and a few dozen well-placed nodes instead of millions. That is within the engineering trajectory already visible in current SBSP and lunar power concepts.
The practical near-term version is a solar-system-scale power and data backbone: collectors closer to the Sun, relays at strategic points, and local receivers wherever people or machines are working. That is the infrastructure that makes the next wave of bases and remote operations feasible without jumping to Dyson-scale or interstellar fantasies.
